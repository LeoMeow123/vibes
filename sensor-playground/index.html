Three-Layer Classification Model

Behavior detection requires three dimensions of information, not just threshold values.

Layer 1: Static Values

Instantaneous sensor readings. Separates intensity levels but cannot distinguish behaviors within the same level.
HR T SpO2
if HR < 90 → low intensity
if HR 90-130 → moderate
if HR > 130 → high
Limitation: eating and office work both have HR 60-90. Cannot separate them with static values alone.

Layer 2: Dynamic Patterns

How values change over a time window (5-10s). Periodicity, frequency, and amplitude distinguish same-intensity behaviors.
θ pattern ω rhythm F envelope
periodicity = std(peak_intervals) / mean(peak_intervals)
if periodicity < 0.3 → rhythmic motion
dominant_freq = FFT peak frequency
This is what separates eating (theta cycles at 0.3 Hz) from office work (theta stable).

Layer 3: Trend Detection

Rate of change over time. Detects transitions and anomalies that single-frame values cannot catch.
dω/dt dF/dt HR trend
rate = (ω_now - ω_prev) / Δt
if rate < -100 deg/s² AND HR > 130
→ FATIGUE WARNING
Fatigue cannot be detected from any single instant. It requires knowing that omega was high and suddenly dropped.

Walking: ω(t) = A·|sin(2πf·t)| + noise   (f ≈ 1.5 Hz, step rhythm)
Eating: θ(t) = θ0 + A·sin(2πf·t)   (f ≈ 0.3 Hz, hand-to-mouth)

Key separator: dominant frequency
f > 0.8 Hz → walking  |  f < 0.5 Hz → eating
Both show periodic motion, but at very different frequencies. Walking is ~1.5 Hz (steps), eating is ~0.3 Hz (arm cycles). The physical reason: walking involves the full body oscillation cycle, eating only the forearm lever.

2. Aerobic vs Strength Training

Where does the force threshold separate aerobic from resistance?
25
120
Joint torque: M = F × L × sin(θ)
For L=0.27m, θ=90°: M = F × 0.27 N·m

Aerobic zone: HR > 100, F < 25 N (M < 6.75 N·m)
Strength zone: HR > 120, F > 25 N (M > 6.75 N·m)

Physical basis: resistance training requires sustained muscle force
against external load. Aerobic keeps HR high but low peak force.
The F threshold comes from physics: at F=25N with L=0.27m, joint torque is ~6.75 N·m. Below this, the elbow moves freely (aerobic). Above it, the muscle is working against resistance (strength).

stability = std(θ) over window of N seconds

Office: θ ∈ [70°, 100°] AND stability < 5°
Resting: θ ∈ [20°, 60°] AND stability < 8°

Both are "still" but at different arm positions.
The desk constrains the elbow to ~85° (typing posture).
Rest lets the arm hang naturally at ~30-40°.
The key differentiator is NOT motion (both are still) but the static angle range. Office = arm on desk (70-100°). Rest = arm relaxed (20-60°). Stability (low std dev) confirms it's sustained, not transitioning.

FATIGUE if ALL of:
  1. ω was > 100 deg/s in last N seconds
  2. ω dropped to < 20 deg/s
  3. |dω/dt| > threshold (sudden, not gradual)
  4. HR still > 130 (body still stressed)

Normal stop: ω decreases gradually over 2-3s
Fatigue: ω drops to ~0 within 0.3-0.5s
The physics: muscle failure means the motor neurons stop firing abruptly. Unlike a deliberate stop (gradual deceleration), fatigue causes near-instantaneous force loss. The rate of omega change (dω/dt) is the critical discriminator, not omega itself.

Key Formulas

Temperature (NTC):

R_T = R_ref × (V_cc / V_out - 1)   |   1/T = 1/T0 + (1/B) × ln(R_T / R0)

Joint Torque:

M = F × L × sin(θ)   |   F=load(N), L=forearm(m)

Impact Force:

α = Δω / Δt   |   M_impact = I × α   |   I = (1/3) × m × L²

SpO2:

R = (AC_red/DC_red) / (AC_IR/DC_IR)   |   SpO2 = 110 - 25 × R
